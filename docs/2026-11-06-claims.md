# Claims

Written on 2026-11-06. What a claim about a harness is, what makes one true, and what that rests on. It is meant to become a section of the Pata spec. Tracked by [CRO-60](https://linear.app/cronch-ai/issue/CRO-60).

## Status

Agreed by the owner in discussion on 2026-11-06:

- A claim is an **address** and a **statement** about what is there.
- **One statement language** for properties, rules and invariants.
- **Three verdicts:** proved, refuted, vouched.
- **What a proof rests on is other claims.**
- **Merkle trees** for addresses and for the claim graph.
- **Qualifiers such as depth and alphabet belong in the statement.**
- **ASCII names** for temporal operators, with a translation table.
- **Sign and revoke** for vouches.

Everything below that says how is proposed, and not yet seen by the owner.

## Why

There are three ways to a guarantee today, and they share no type:

- **Evidence** ([evidence.rs](../crates/kernel/src/evidence.rs)): the kernel's own verdict on a property. It replays a counterexample, searches to a depth, explores every configuration, or checks a certificate.
- **Induction** ([induction.rs](../crates/kernel/src/induction.rs)): the kernel decides initiation, and a solver searches for a counterexample to consecution, which the kernel checks.
- **Rules** ([rules.rs](../crates/kernel/src/rules.rs)): the kernel writes queries, and a proof checked outside the kernel shows them `unsat`. A checked proof produces no evidence.

F2 says claims are first-class. These are three mechanisms with no claim among them.

## The shape

A claim is an address and a statement. Its key is the hash of both, so the same claim has the same key wherever it is made.

A verdict is about a claim, and is one of:

- **Proved:** a proof, the checker that accepts it, and the claims it rests on.
- **Refuted:** a counterexample the kernel replays.
- **Vouched:** a signature over the claim's key, by whoever stands behind it.

A claim with no verdict is **open**. A failed attempt to prove it can be attached to it, and is still not a verdict. See [what is not a verdict](#what-is-not-a-verdict).

## Addresses

### Merkle keys

Today a key is the SHA-256 of an object's whole deterministic CBOR ([key.rs](../crates/kernel/src/key.rs)). A harness's key says nothing of its parts.

With Merkle keys, a composite's key is the hash of its kind, its version and its children's keys. An address is a root key and the paths of the parts the statement reads. An inclusion proof, the keys along each path, shows that a part is in a root, without the rest of the root.

The tree, as a first cut:

- **Harness:** its signature (`arg`, result), state type, initial state, step and each declared child.
- **Interface:** each capability by prefix, then each operation and channel, then the parts of its contract.
- **Contract parts:**

| Part         | Fields                                       | Read when                        |
| ------------ | -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Signature    | `input`, `output`, `mode`                    | typing                           |
| Gate         | `pre`                                        | at each call                     |
| Admission    | `post`, or a channel's `inv`                 | when a result or message arrives |
| Provenance   | `ret`, `raise`, or a channel's `label`       | labelling what arrives           |
| Effect       | `effect`, `footprint`                        | recording on the trace           |
| Grant update | `onCall`, `onResult`, or a channel's `onMsg` | changing the grant               |

The grant type's fields are leaves too, since a rule reads some of them and not others.

Keys already start with a kind and version, so Merkle keys are a new version of each kind, not a silent change. They still change every stored key, including the platform's.

### When a claim about parts holds of a whole

A claim names the parts it reads. A verdict on it carries over to any whole that contains those parts unchanged, but only if nothing else in that whole can change what the statement reads. The rest of the whole must be **framed**.

`ruleObligations` already checks this. An update that writes none of the fields a rule's precondition reads is listed as `framed`, and its query is unchanged to the byte. One that writes them owes a new obligation.

So a verdict carries over to a new whole when:

1. the parts it reads are in the whole, by inclusion proof; and
2. every other part is framed against them, which the kernel decides from the parts themselves.

That is T1's "adding a capability re-proves nothing". A capability that is framed costs a frame check. One that isn't changes what the claim reads, so the claim changes, and it owes new proof of the new part only.

Framing is easy to decide for a rule, which reads named grant fields. For a property of a harness, which reads the whole of its behaviour, the address is the harness root and the interface's contracts. Claims about part of a harness, such as one child, are [open](#open).

## Statements

### One language

A statement is a formula in the property language of [property.rs](../crates/kernel/src/property.rs), with `always` on top. Formulas are written informally here: their terms are the property language's. The address says which runs it ranges over:

| Address                         | Ranges over                            | Example                                            |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| A harness, against an interface | that harness's runs                    | `always(not(call(shell.run)))`                     |
| An interface, or parts of one   | every harness's runs against it        | `always(implies(call(net.fetch), once(approved)))` |
| A harness's state               | that harness's runs, read at its state | `always(p(state))`                                 |

Rules and invariants become formulas at an address:

- **An invariant** reads `state`, which every `out` event's view carries ([view.rs](../crates/kernel/src/view.rs)).
- **A rule** is a formula at an interface address. Its queries become one way to prove that formula, beside search and certificates. The formula is about events, and the queries are about the grant, so a rule's proof shows the formula only through a correspondence: the interpreter checks the gate at every call. That correspondence is an [open](#open) question: whether it is proved once, or rests on a vouched claim.

### Qualifiers

A statement can be qualified: `within(depth, alphabet, formula)`. Bounded search then proves a weaker statement, rather than being a weaker kind of proof. Exhaustive search and certificates prove `within(alphabet, formula)`.

The alphabet is an assumption today (`Assumption::Environment`). As a qualifier it is part of what was shown, and no longer something it rests on.

### ASCII

The property language already uses ASCII names. Only the docs write `□`. Docs and the spec use the names, with this table for readers who know the symbols:

| Pata              | Symbol  | Holds at an event when                                             |
| ----------------- | ------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `always(φ)`       | □φ      | φ holds at every event (the top of every statement)                |
| `prev(φ)`         | ●φ, Y φ | φ held at the previous event                                       |
| `once(φ)`         | ◆φ, P φ | φ held at some event up to this one                                |
| `historically(φ)` | ■φ, H φ | φ held at every event up to this one                               |
| `since(φ, ψ)`     | φ S ψ   | ψ held at some event up to this one, and φ at every event after it |

## Verdicts

### Proved

A proof names its checker and the claims it rests on. The kernel is one checker, and cvc5's proofs checked by Ethos are another. That the checker is sound is itself a claim, usually vouched. The kernel's own soundness is `Assumption::Checker` today, and Verus proofs over the interpreter would make it proved (F7).

The kernel and Ethos then differ only in which claims a proof rests on, and the verdict has the same shape.

### Refuted

A counterexample is a sequence of deliveries that the kernel replays to a violation. For an interface address, it also names the harness. A refutation of a qualified statement uses only inputs from its alphabet.

**A false safety claim always has one.** A violation of `always(φ)` has a finite prefix that breaks it. The model is adversarial (F3), so any well-typed output is an admissible input, and the prefix is a run that can happen. Finding it is only semi-decidable, but it exists.

- **Two-run properties,** such as non-interference, are refuted by a pair of finite runs.
- **Liveness** isn't, in general: a violation is an infinite run. Liveness is a guarantee only where it is claimed (F8), and such a claim is proved, vouched or open.

### Vouched

A vouch is a signature, by a key, over a claim's key. Signatures sit beside objects, never inside them, as [key.rs](../crates/kernel/src/key.rs) already says, so vouching doesn't change a key.

These are vouched today, as assumptions:

| Today                                                    | As a claim                                                                           |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| A1 `Contracts`: implementations keep their contracts     | each capability's contracts are kept, vouched by its publisher                       |
| A2 `Checker`: the checker is sound                       | vouched by the checker's authors, or proved                                          |
| A3 `Runtime`: the runtime conforms to the interpreter    | the host keeps the [host protocol](2026-11-06-host-protocol.md), vouched by the host |
| `Environment`: the environment chooses from the alphabet | not a claim: a [qualifier](#qualifiers)                                              |

### What is not a verdict

- **A counterexample to induction.** A `sat` consecution query gives a state where the step breaks the invariant. That state may be unreachable, so this shows a failed proof, not a false claim. `checkInduction` already says `notInductive`, never "violated".
- **A model of a rule's query.** A `sat` query shows that the gate's invariant isn't preserved, which may also be unreachable.
- **A solver's bare `unsat`,** a timeout, or an unknown.

These can be attached to an open claim as attempts.

## The claim graph

Claims and verdicts are content-addressed, and a proof names the claims it rests on by key. They form a Merkle DAG, as git commits do. There can be no cycles, since a node's key covers the keys it names.

- **A bundle** is a verdict and every node it reaches. Anyone can check it offline, with no part of Cronch (F6).
- **A verdict's trusted base** is the vouched claims it reaches. F7's "the trusted base is explicit" is then a walk of the graph.
- **A3's "a child's claims entail its parent's rules"** is one more proved claim, whose statement relates two claims.

## Signing and revocation

Vouches are signed, and a signer can revoke one. Content-addressed nodes can't change, so revocation can't live in the graph.

- **A revocation** is a signed object naming a vouch, or naming a key, which revokes every vouch it made.
- **Revocations go in an append-only, hash-chained log,** as a run's log does already. A log head is signed.
- **A verdict's status is read against a log head:** "proved, as of head _h_". A proved verdict that reaches a vouch revoked by _h_ is open again at _h_. Finding the verdicts a revocation reaches is a walk of the graph.
- **Who may revoke:** the signer, for its own vouches, and the key's owner, for the key.

The format defines the revocation object and how status is read (F1). Keeping a log is a service anyone can run. Cronch would run one, and a verdict names the log it was read against.

## Order

1. **Merkle keys for interfaces and contracts.** T1's second step needs them: adding a capability and showing what is re-proved ([CRO-55](https://linear.app/cronch-ai/issue/CRO-55)).
2. **Claim and verdict objects,** with today's evidence mapped onto them.
3. **Rules as a way to prove a formula** at an interface address.
4. **Vouches and the revocation log,** in the platform.

## Open

- **The frame condition beyond rules.** For a rule it is "writes none of the fields the gate reads". What it is for a property of a harness isn't worked out.
- **Claims about part of a harness.** Whether a claim about one child, or one step's branch, carries over when the rest of the harness changes.
- **Entailment.** A3 needs a statement that relates claims, which `always` can't say. It is probably the one form the language needs beyond formulas.
- **Rule and formula.** Whether the correspondence between a rule's queries and its formula is proved once, as a lemma, or rests on a vouched claim.
- **The cost of Merkle keys.** Every stored key changes, including evidence's, and the platform's objects would need re-keying or both versions read.
- **How fresh a log head must be.** A verifier offline can't know of revocations after the head it has.
